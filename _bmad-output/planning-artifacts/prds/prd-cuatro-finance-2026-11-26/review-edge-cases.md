# Review: edge cases

## Verdict

The PRD pins down more money rules than most (the TRM by validity range, pending rates, half-even rounding, month-end clamping, a match window), but its rules for telling movements apart stop at the simple case: one account, one currency, and a capture that a single import later confirms. Built as written, an own-account card payment imported from both accounts posts twice, cuota purchases already inside an opening balance or repeated on later statements post again, and the forecast and the payment plan count amounts twice or on no account, because no recurring occurrence is tied to the transaction that fulfills it and no liability names the account that pays it. None of the 39 findings (1 critical, 16 high, 18 medium, 4 low) asks for a feature outside the MVP scope; each asks for the rule that decides a case the FRs already reach.

## Findings

Each finding gives its severity, its location, the input that breaks or leaves behavior undefined, and what the requirement should add. Examples use the fictional Mariana and made-up numbers in es-CO format.

### Critical

**EC-1 (critical). FR-28, FR-26, §5.4, UJ-5: a transaction that came from an import is never a match candidate.**
- Input: Mariana pays her card COP 2.000.000 from her COP savings account on the Friday before a Monday holiday. She imports the savings export first; its row becomes a transfer, so the app creates the card side with source import, dated Friday. The card statement, imported later, shows the payment on Tuesday under another description. It is not a duplicate (date and description differ), not a near duplicate (date differs), and not a match, because FR-28 offers matches only against transactions captured by Claude or by hand. It posts, and the card's debt falls by COP 4.000.000, against §5.4's promise that the same transaction never posts twice. The same rule posts twice a movement imported from two files of one account (a PDF statement Claude read, then the XLS export) that date or describe it differently. Nothing says whether the duplicate check compares the statement row that a match recorded, so re-importing a matched row can offer it as a match again.
- Add: match candidates are every transaction on the account that no statement row has confirmed yet, whatever its source, a transfer side the app generated included; a transaction already confirmed by a statement row is a duplicate of that row on re-import, never a second match; and Open Question 3 tests the 3-day window (A-5) on transfer sides across holiday weekends, since Friday to Tuesday is already 4 days.

### High

**EC-2 (high). Glossary "Account", R-4, FR-20, FR-21, FR-29, FR-36, FR-39, FR-56: one currency per account against a card's USD sub-balance and a crypto account with several assets.**
- Input: Mariana's card carries a USD sub-balance (A-3) and she tells Claude "USD 20 on my card". FR-56 takes the currency from the account and FR-20 gives every transaction its account's currency, so the charge records in COP. A crypto account holding two assets has two positions (FR-21) but one currency. The card statement then has a closing balance per currency, and may show a minimum per currency, while FR-29 takes one closing balance and FR-39 schedules one minimum.
- Add: a transaction carries the currency of the sub-balance it moves (or names its crypto asset), not only the account's; reconciliation takes one closing balance per sub-balance; minimum payments, plan lines, and debt totals keep each sub-balance in its own currency and convert it for COP totals at the TRM in force (EC-27); matching stays within one sub-balance; and the payoff order uses the USD sub-balance's own rate when the card terms give one.

**EC-3 (high). FR-18, FR-27, FR-29, FR-36, UJ-1: amounts already inside an opening balance post again.**
- Input: in UJ-1, Mariana opens her card with a debt of COP 3.000.000 that already includes a running purchase of COP 1.200.000 in 6 cuotas, 2 of them paid. Recorded like any purchase (FR-36), it raises the debt by COP 1.200.000 more. Separately, her opening date is 10 November and her first statement covers 26 October to 25 November: the rows dated up to 10 November are already in the opening balance, match nothing, and post again.
- Add: a running cuota purchase entered at setup records its remaining principal and cuotas without changing the balance; the opening balance is the balance at the end of its date, and staged rows dated on or before it are listed and skipped like duplicates (the owner can still post one); and the first reconciliation period starts the day after the opening date.

**EC-4 (high). FR-27, FR-28, FR-33, FR-36: a statement row for a later cuota of a purchase already in the ledger.**
- Input: card statements commonly list every running purchase again each period with its cuota number. Next month, the laptop stand from UJ-4 appears as "cuota 2 de 6" with that cuota's value and interest. Its amount and date differ from the purchase in the ledger and no capture sits within 3 days, so it posts as new spending and raises the debt again. In the other direction, the column mapping holds only date, description, and amount, so a cuota purchase imported from XLS or CSV cannot record its number of cuotas and gets no installment schedule.
- Add: a staged row recognized as cuota n of an existing purchase links to it, records that cuota as billed and the interest it charged (which posts as interest spending), and never posts as a purchase; the column mapping can name the cuota count and cuota number columns; and the PRD states that a cuota purchase counts once, in full, in its purchase month's budget, as FR-33 implies today.

**EC-5 (high). FR-33, FR-36, FR-38, FR-47, R-6: a refunded or reversed purchase.**
- Input: Mariana returns a COP 900.000 jacket bought in 3 cuotas; the next statement shows a credit of COP 900.000 and the pending cuotas end. Nothing links a credit to the purchase it reverses, so the schedule keeps producing cuotas, and the minimum estimate (R-6), the plan, and the forecast keep counting them. Nothing says whether a credit filed under a spending category lowers that category's spending, in which month when it lands after the month closed, or counts as income in FR-47. A refund after the card is paid off leaves a credit balance that the payoff comparison and the plan do not define.
- Add: a credit can be marked as reversing a purchase, which ends that purchase's remaining cuotas; a credit in a spending category lowers that category's spending in one stated month (its own or the purchase's) and is never income; and a liability with a credit balance has no minimum payment and leaves the payoff order.

**EC-6 (high). FR-36, FR-38, FR-39, FR-40, R-5: a card payment above the minimum.**
- Input: UJ-4 sends COP 300.000 above the minimum to a card with three running cuota purchases and a revolving balance. The extra payment rule covers only loans and the mortgage (glossary), so nothing says which balance the extra lowers, or whether it shortens a purchase's remaining cuotas or lowers each cuota. The next minimum estimate (R-6) and UJ-4's "7 months sooner" both depend on it. Changing a purchase's number of cuotas, a write FR-54 allows, has the same gap.
- Add: the card terms (R-5) say how a payment above the minimum spreads across the card's balances and whether it shortens cuotas or lowers them; the payoff comparison and the scenarios apply that rule; and every schedule recomputes from its remaining principal after such a payment or a change in cuotas.

**EC-7 (high). FR-26, FR-37, R-3: a loan payment that is not the scheduled installment.**
- Input: Mariana's car loan installment of COP 1.150.000 includes COP 35.000 of insurance the app does not know about. One month she pays COP 1.000.000, the next nothing, and the third COP 2.500.000, four days after the due day. FR-37 stores debt, rate, installment, and remaining term, which determine each other and disagree once the installment holds charges. Nothing defines the split of a short, missed, double, early, or late payment, interest for part of a month (R-3 gives only a monthly rate and no day count, so leap years have no rule either), or which figure wins when the lender's statement splits a payment differently. The debt drifts from the lender's and the loan never reconciles (SM-1).
- Add: loan terms include the fixed charges inside the installment; a payment covers charges and interest due first, then principal, then any excess under the extra payment rule; a short or missed payment leaves the rest due and is flagged; interest for part of a month uses a stated day count; the schedule recomputes from the current debt after every payment; and the lender's split, once the owner or Claude enters it, replaces the computed one as an audited write.

**EC-8 (high). R-6, FR-29, FR-36, FR-39, FR-41, FR-48, FR-54: the statement's minimum and due date, and shifted due dates.**
- Input: R-6 makes the statement's minimum payment the figure, and the statement prints the real payment due date, but no FR or write tool captures either: FR-29 and FR-54 take only the closing balance and date, and a movements export carries neither, so after UJ-4 the plan still shows the estimate. Separately, a computed due date of Sunday 1 March 2026 with a previous-business-day shift lands on Friday 27 February. Nothing says whether the plan's "due this month" (FR-39), the reminders (A-9), the forecast, and the "before the next income event" flag read the shifted or the nominal date, so the payment can miss February's plan and appear in March's already past. A due date derived from card or loan terms also has no stated source for its shift.
- Add: statement entry takes the minimum payment (per sub-balance, EC-2) and the printed due date, which override the computed ones for that period; a derived due date takes its shift from the account's terms, defaulting to the previous business day; and every consumer reads the shifted date, which also places the payment in its month.

**EC-9 (high). FR-36, FR-37, FR-39, FR-43, FR-44: no liability names the account that pays it.**
- Input: Mariana has two COP savings accounts and a USD account, each with a cash buffer. FR-39 checks "the paying account" against its buffer and FR-44 places minimum payments and installments on accounts, but no FR says which account pays a card, a loan, or the mortgage, or which one funds the monthly extra. The forecast cannot place the outflow, and the buffer check has no account to check.
- Add: each liability names its paying account (for a USD sub-balance, also the currency it is paid in), the monthly extra names its funding account, the plan and the forecast use them, and a payment recorded from another account still fulfills the plan line (EC-10).

**EC-10 (high). FR-35, FR-39, FR-41, FR-42, FR-44, FR-48: no recurring occurrence is tied to the transaction that fulfills it.**
- Input: the card minimum due on the 15th is paid on the 10th; the electricity bill posts two days after its date; this month's goal contribution was moved by hand on the 3rd; a confirmed subscription is not charged this month. With no link from an occurrence to a transaction, the forecast still projects the payment on the 15th, counting it twice; the plan lists it as due and its reminder fires; the contribution counts as progress only if someone marks it; and a missed payment or charge goes unnoticed.
- Add: a posted transaction fulfills the open occurrence of a recurring event on its account (amount within a stated tolerance, date within a stated window), automatically or through the review queue; a fulfilled occurrence stops projecting, shows as paid in the plan, and silences its reminders; a transfer that fulfills a planned contribution is that goal's contribution; and an occurrence whose date passes unfulfilled is flagged (a missed payment for SM-3, a skipped charge for FR-42) and never projected into the past.

**EC-11 (high). FR-44: where and when variable estimates land.**
- Input: Restaurantes is projected at COP 600.000 a month, and Mariana pays restaurants from her card and from her savings. Projected onto the card, the estimate never reaches cash, since only the card's payments leave savings; projected onto savings, the same spending reaches cash a second time through the card's next payment. Nothing says how a monthly estimate spreads over its days (all on the 1st, or evenly), which moves the lowest balance, or whether the current month nets out what is already posted: on the 20th, with COP 450.000 posted, netting adds COP 150.000 and not netting adds 600.000.
- Add: each category's estimate lands on accounts by its recent split across them; projected card spending reaches cash only through the card's projected payments, under a stated rule (statement balance or minimum); a monthly estimate spreads evenly over the month's remaining days; and the current month projects only what remains above the posted amount, never below zero.

**EC-12 (high). FR-42, FR-44: category averages that repeat recurring events.**
- Input: the confirmed music subscription, the electricity bill (a recurring event), and the car loan's interest postings (installments) all carry categories, so their past transactions also sit in each category's 3-month or 12-month average. FR-44 projects the event (high) and the category average (medium or low) on top of it, so each counts twice; a yearly insurance entered as an event also shows up as irregular spending in its category.
- Add: category averages exclude transactions that fulfilled a recurring event, a subscription, or an installment (EC-10), so each future movement is projected once, from its most certain source.

**EC-13 (high). FR-44, glossary "Confidence level": less history than the look-back windows.**
- Input: in the first months after setup, the months SM-1 and SM-2 measure, the ledger covers 2 months. Every category then has spending in fewer than 3 of the last 6 months, so all are irregular and projected at a 12-month average that divides 2 months of spending by 12. The forecast shows a fraction of real spending, and the buffer checks behind UJ-4 and UJ-8 pass when they should not.
- Add: averages divide by the months the ledger covers (since its earliest opening date); a category is irregular only once the ledger covers 6 months; and a category with under a month of history projects its budget if it has one, or shows as unestimated (low) rather than as zero.

**EC-14 (high). FR-35, FR-40, FR-41, UJ-8: no one-off planned event and no change to a single occurrence.**
- Input: UJ-8's edge case moves one month's COP 750.000 contribution into the months around it (0 in June, 1.125.000 in May and in July), and FR-40 compares "a one-off amount". A recurring event has one amount and one frequency, so the split cannot be stored, and a chosen one-off payment has nowhere the plan and the forecast would read it (FR-40 saves only the monthly extra). UJ-8's end-to-end check (NFR-10) has nothing to assert.
- Add: a recurring event can change or skip a single occurrence; a one-off planned event (amount, account, date) exists for the plan and the forecast; and FR-40 can save a one-off option as one.

**EC-15 (high). FR-20, FR-26, FR-28, R-2, UJ-6: transfers across currencies.**
- Input: Mariana converts COP 4.200.000 into USD 1.000 on a day the TRM is 4.150,00. R-2 locks the USD side at the TRM (COP 4.150.000) while FR-26 stores the implied rate of 4.200,00, so COP 50.000 of spread is neither spending nor income nor shown, and history at locked rates shows the pair off by that much. In UJ-6 she links an unmatched COP debit to her USD account before that account's file arrives: the generated USD side has no defined amount, since the bank's rate is not known yet, and the real USD row, arriving with another amount, never meets FR-28's "same amount", so it posts too (EC-1). Paying a card's USD sub-balance from a COP account carries the same spread.
- Add: the generated side of a cross-currency transfer stays pending (amount unknown, outside balances) until its own row arrives or the owner supplies the amount; a cross-currency transfer candidate is found by date window and a plausible implied rate, not by equal amounts; and the PRD states whether a conversion's spread against the TRM is shown (for example, as a line on the transfer) or deliberately ignored.

**EC-16 (high). FR-25, FR-28, FR-29, FR-55, R-2: reconciliation by date, and later writes into a reconciled period.**
- Input: Mariana tells Claude about a COP 85.000 purchase on 25 November, her card's cut day; the bank processes it on the 26th and bills it on the next statement. FR-29 compares the closing balance with the app's balance on the closing date, which includes it, so the statement is off by COP 85.000 and never marked reconciled, and this recurs whenever a capture sits near a cut day. FR-28 records a match's statement date "with" the transaction without saying whether it replaces the date that budgets, balances, and the locked rate use (R-2 re-locks on a date correction). Separately, once November is marked reconciled, an undo, a void, an edit, a date correction, or a backdated import inside that period leaves the mark in place.
- Add: a transaction is cleared once a statement row confirms it, keeping its own date for budgets and its locked rate, while the statement's date decides the period it clears in; reconciliation compares the closing balance with the cleared balance and lists the period's uncleared transactions as pending, not as a difference; and a write that changes a cleared transaction in a reconciled period warns first (FR-55) and removes the mark until the statement reconciles again.

**EC-17 (high). FR-46, R-2, NFR-11: balance snapshots against backdated writes.**
- Input: the December card statement arrives on 6 January and posts 14 rows dated in December that nobody told Claude about; voids, undos, and date corrections also land on past dates. Snapshots recorded once a day are then wrong from the earliest of those dates, so net worth history disagrees with the ledger, and the year-end snapshot kept for the deferred tax features misstates 31 December. A day on which the snapshot job failed has no snapshot at all.
- Add: a write dated before today recomputes the snapshots from its date onward (balances from the ledger, each day at the TRM in force and the price stored for that date), or history is derived from the ledger and snapshots keep only each day's rates and prices; missed days backfill the same way; and the year-end snapshot shows when it was last recomputed.

### Medium

**EC-18 (medium). R-1, FR-11, FR-54, NFR-1: input with excess decimals or malformed groups.**
- Input: Claude declares es-CO and sends "48,000" meaning forty-eight thousand. Under es-CO that reads as 48 with three decimals, which is not ambiguous, so rounding on store (NFR-1) keeps COP 48. "6.99" for a USD amount, read by a parser that drops thousands separators, becomes USD 699. A crypto quantity with more decimals than its asset allows has the same gap.
- Add: an amount with more decimals than its currency's minor unit, or than its asset's precision, is rejected, never rounded; a thousands separator not followed by exactly three digits is rejected; both cases name the reason (FR-54) and sit in the money layer's tests (NFR-10).

**EC-19 (medium). FR-29, NFR-1, NFR-2, R-1: rounding twice, and totals that do not add up.**
- Input: a computed COP value of 10.001,4951 rounds half-even to 10.001,50 when stored and to 10.002 when shown, while a single rounding gives 10.001. Three converted amounts stored at COP 0,50 each show as 0, 0, and 0 beside a total of 2. A whole-peso statement never equals a balance holding centavos from app-computed interest, so FR-29's zero difference fails by a fraction of a peso.
- Add: state whether "one point" allows the display rounding of a stored centavo value (accepting a one-peso difference) or COP is computed in whole pesos; state that a shown total rounds the exact sum, not the shown rows; and compare reconciliation at the statement's precision.

**EC-20 (medium). FR-35, FR-36, FR-37, FR-44, NFR-10: residues when an amount is divided.**
- Input: COP 100.000 in 3 cuotas is 33.333,33 each, which sums to 99.999,99. A loan's last installment, a goal's needed contribution of COP 1.000.000 over 3 months, and a 12-month average spread across days leave residues too, and NFR-10's external fixtures only agree with the code if both use the same residue rule.
- Add: whenever the money layer divides an amount, the parts sum exactly to the whole, with the residue on a stated part (the last cuota, installment, or contribution), and the fixtures follow that rule.

**EC-21 (medium). FR-21, NFR-1, addendum §3.6: crypto precision, prices, and cost basis.**
- Input: a holding of 150,123456789012345678 units of an 18-decimal asset has 21 significant digits, beyond the precision of 20 the addendum sets for decimal.js; a token priced at USD 0,00001234 becomes zero if prices are held in USD cents. Selling 0,005 units of a crypto asset from a position of 0,0125 units has no cost basis method (average cost, or first in, first out), so the remaining basis and the realized gain are undefined, and as a transfer the sale shows its gain nowhere. A move between two crypto accounts that pays a network fee in the asset arrives short of what left.
- Add: state the magnitude and precision that a crypto quantity and a price must hold exactly; one cost basis method; a sale shows its realized gain or loss, outside spending and income; and a fee paid in the asset posts as spending of that asset.

**EC-22 (medium). FR-28, FR-56, A-5: ties and the window's edge.**
- Input: Mariana told Claude about two COP 15.000 taxis, on Monday and Wednesday; the statement has rows on Tuesday and Thursday. Each row is within 3 days of both captures, and nothing says which pairs with which, or that a capture matches one row only; two equal card payments in a month, as transfer candidates, raise the same question. "Within 3 days" also does not say calendar or business days, or whether a gap of exactly 3 days is inside, and a purchase on Holy Wednesday that the bank processes the next Monday is 5 calendar days out.
- Add: matching is one to one, nearest date first, then the earliest capture; the window is stated in inclusive calendar days, or in business days, which absorbs holiday weeks; and Open Question 3 tests holiday weeks.

**EC-23 (medium). FR-25, FR-26, FR-28, FR-55, glossary "Void": voided records in later checks.**
- Input: Mariana voids a Claude transaction she believes is a mistake, and the statement then brings that very charge. If voided records take part in duplicate and match checks, the row is skipped against a record that no longer counts; if they do not, undoing the void later leaves two. Voiding a transaction already matched to a statement row drops the statement's row from the ledger, but only undo warns about that (FR-55, UJ-6). FR-25 keeps a transfer pair consistent on edit, not when one side is voided.
- Add: voided records never count in duplicate and match checks; undoing a void that a later row replaced warns and offers the match; voiding a matched transaction warns as undo does and returns its statement row to the review queue; and voiding either side of a transfer voids the pair.

**EC-24 (medium). FR-30, FR-31, FR-55, A-10: undo against later writes and writes that change many records.**
- Input: Claude corrects a lunch from COP 48.000 to 58.000 (write 1), Mariana then recategorizes it (write 2), and she undoes write 1. If undo restores write 1's whole before-record, her category reverts too. Undoing an import confirmation (29 posted rows and 3 matches), a category merge (FR-30 moved every transaction, rule, and budget), or a rule (and the transactions it has categorized since) is undefined as one undo or many, and against records changed since.
- Add: undo restores only the fields its write changed, and asks when a later write changed the same field; a write that changed many records undoes as one unit, listing what it will change before applying.

**EC-25 (medium). FR-41, FR-42: subscriptions that change price, bill in another currency, or skip a month.**
- Input: a subscription moves from COP 26.900 to 29.900 in the fourth of six months. The average is 28.400 and every charge sits more than 5 percent from it, so detection never proposes it. A USD-priced service billed in COP drifts with the TRM by more than 5 percent over 6 months. A monthly charge that skips once has intervals of 30, 30, 61, and 31 days, far above a 3-day standard deviation. For a confirmed subscription, nothing says whether a charge more than 5 percent off is still attached to it (and flagged) or treated as unrelated, whether the expected amount follows an accepted price change or every later charge flags, or what a missed charge does; and a subscription renewing every 3 or 6 months can be added only as a yearly one.
- Add: detection compares amounts in the charge's own currency where the statement gives it, accepts one price step (charges within 5 percent of the price since the step), and counts an interval near a multiple of the base one; a confirmed subscription attaches charges by merchant, flags a price change, and adopts the new amount once the owner or Claude accepts it; a missed charge is flagged (EC-10); and an added subscription can take any FR-41 frequency.

**EC-26 (medium). FR-35, A-7, UJ-8: goal withdrawals, the purchase, and cycles.**
- Input: Mariana moves COP 1.000.000 out of her pocket for an emergency; progress counts contributions in, so "Portátil nuevo" still shows the full amount. When she buys the laptop from the pocket, nothing completes the goal. A sinking fund whose target date passes COP 200.000 over or short has no rule for the remainder, and its repeat period is undefined; a plain goal whose date passes unmet has no state. Had she chosen to buy USD each month (UJ-8), nothing says whether progress toward a COP target uses each contribution's locked rate or the TRM in force.
- Add: a transfer out of the funding account can be marked as a withdrawal from the goal, which lowers progress; spending from that account can be marked as the goal's purchase, which completes the goal; a sinking fund has a repeat period and carries its remainder into the next cycle; a passed target date shows as missed, with the shortfall; and progress in another currency uses one stated valuation.

**EC-27 (medium). FR-20, FR-45, FR-46, NFR-8, R-2: "latest TRM" and staleness.**
- Input: the TRM is published the business day before it takes effect, so after an evening fetch the latest stored TRM is tomorrow's, and "current balances convert at the latest TRM" values today's dashboard, today's snapshot, and the 31 December snapshot at the next day's rate. The other way round, one TRM can stay in force for several days: on the Tuesday after a Monday holiday, the TRM in force has been valid since Saturday, and NFR-8's "older than 2 days" labels it stale.
- Add: "latest TRM" means the TRM in force on today's Bogotá date; a snapshot uses the TRM in force on its own date; a TRM is stale only when no stored TRM covers the date it is used for; and the "older than 2 days" rule applies to prices.

**EC-28 (medium). FR-20, FR-23, R-2: a manual TRM, then the official one.**
- Input: the TRM fetch fails before a long weekend; the owner enters a manual TRM for Saturday (FR-20's last resort) and three USD transactions lock at it; on Tuesday the official TRM covering that Saturday arrives with another value. R-2 says each date has exactly one TRM in force and that only a correction changes a locked rate, so both cannot hold. A manual UVR (FR-23) has the same conflict.
- Add: one stated rule: either the official value supersedes the manual one and the affected transactions re-lock as audited writes (an exception written into R-2), or the manual value stands and the official one is recorded beside it.

**EC-29 (medium). FR-38, FR-39, FR-43: the payment plan when cash is short.**
- Input: the minimum payments due before Mariana's next income event total COP 2.100.000, and the paying account holds COP 1.500.000. FR-39 keeps every minimum and flags a buffer breach, but a balance below zero (a payment that cannot be made) gets the same flag, and nothing orders the minimums to cover first. The plan does not say whether the buffer limits the extra on the payment day only or across the next 90 days, as UJ-4's scenario does. A monthly amount for debt below the sum of the minimums (FR-38), and a plan with no income event at all (FR-39's "before the next income event"), are undefined.
- Add: the plan flags a shortfall (a projected balance below zero on a payment date) apart from a buffer breach, with its amount and in due date order; the extra is limited by the lowest projected balance over the forecast horizon, as in UJ-4; FR-38 rejects a monthly amount below the minimums; and with no income event in the horizon, each payment is flagged as not covered by income.

**EC-30 (medium). FR-38, FR-40: payoff assumptions for a card still in use, and ties.**
- Input: UJ-4's "pays the card off 7 months sooner" holds only if Mariana stops using the card, and nothing says whether payoff schedules assume no new purchases or project card spending. Avalanche with two debts at the same EA rate, and snowball with two equal debts, have no tie-break, so the deterministic plan can order them either way.
- Add: payoff comparisons and scenarios state that they count only the debt outstanding today, with no new purchases; and ties break by a fixed rule (for example, avalanche by smaller debt and snowball by higher rate, then by earlier payment due date).

**EC-31 (medium). FR-40, FR-44, FR-45, FR-53, R-6: the lowest balance and its confidence.**
- Input: FR-40 and FR-45 show "the lowest balance" without saying whether it is per asset account or the COP total, or whether that total includes debts, and UJ-4 compares it with "her cash buffer" while buffers are per account. A day's confidence comes from the events that move it, so day 60, moved only by an installment, shows high although its balance carries 59 days of medium estimates; and FR-44 labels minimum payments high even where R-6 calls them estimates.
- Add: the lowest balance is reported per asset account against its own buffer, with the asset total as a separate figure; a day's confidence is the lowest level of any estimate that feeds its balance from today through that day; and an estimated minimum is medium, a statement minimum high.

**EC-32 (medium). FR-27: what a column mapping must hold.**
- Input: an export with separate debit and credit columns; a card export where purchases are positive and payments negative, beside a savings export with the opposite sign; a file with header lines above the table and a totals or closing balance line below it, which "a row that fails to parse rejects the whole file" turns into a rejected file every time.
- Add: the column mapping holds the sign convention, or separate debit and credit columns, and the leading and trailing lines to skip; only rows inside the mapped table can reject the file.

**EC-33 (medium). FR-30, FR-33, FR-34, SM-4: budget months.**
- Input: a statement imported on 6 January posts December spending after December closed. FR-34 keeps each closed month's actual spending without saying whether it recomputes, so UJ-7's six months may miss late imports, and FR-33 may raise a threshold alert for a closed month. A void that brings spending back under the threshold, followed by a charge that crosses it again, may or may not alert twice. A month with no budget written has no rule: does the last limit carry forward, or is there none, which SM-4 counts as missing? Merging two categories that both have a budget for the same month leaves two limits on one category.
- Add: closed months' actuals come from the ledger, so late imports count; threshold and limit alerts fire only for the current month, once per category and level; a month without a written budget either inherits the last limit or has none, stated, and SM-4 counts accordingly; and a merge sums the two limits or keeps the survivor's, stated.

**EC-34 (medium). FR-36, R-4, R-5: a purchase with its own rate, and a card rate that changes.**
- Input: a 6-cuota purchase at a 0 percent promotional rate, while FR-36 computes every schedule at the card's EA rate, so the minimum estimate and the payoff figures add interest that is never charged. When the owner updates the card's EA rate, nothing says whether the pending cuotas of earlier purchases reprice.

**EC-35 (medium). FR-46, FR-48, FR-54, FR-56, NFR-2, R-9: Bogotá dates at the edges.**
- Input: a job scheduled at 00:05 on a UTC server clock runs at 19:05 of the previous Bogotá day, so the "on the day" reminder (A-9) arrives the evening before and a snapshot taken then misses that evening's transactions. At 20:30 Bogotá time (01:30 UTC the next day) Mariana says "yesterday", and Claude, reasoning in UTC, sends today's Bogotá date, or a timestamp ending in Z whose date part is tomorrow's. An OFX file can give a posting time in GMT whose date part is the next Bogotá day.
- Add: every scheduled job's day is the Bogotá date, and end-of-day jobs (snapshots, month close) run after the Bogotá day ends; the record tool accepts relative dates ("today", "yesterday") and resolves them in Bogotá; and any timestamp with a zone, from Claude or from a file, converts to its Bogotá date.

### Low

**EC-36 (low). FR-41, R-9: recurrence anchors.**
- Input: a monthly event anchored on 31 January and computed from each previous occurrence drifts to the 28th after February; an event on the 1st shifted to Monday the 3rd can move the next month's computation; "twice a month" on the 29th and the 31st clamps both to 28 February; a holiday the owner removes from a past date re-shifts occurrences already fulfilled.
- Add: each occurrence is computed from the anchor, then clamped, then shifted; two days clamped to one date give two occurrences; and a holiday change re-shifts only future occurrences.

**EC-37 (low). FR-47: "the previous period of the same length".**
- Input: for March 2027 (31 days), the previous period of the same length runs 29 January to 28 February, not February.
- Add: a calendar month compares with the previous calendar month, and any other range with the same number of days just before it.

**EC-38 (low). FR-24, FR-52: an amount range across currencies.**

**EC-39 (low). FR-15, R-1: an export rounded for display.**
